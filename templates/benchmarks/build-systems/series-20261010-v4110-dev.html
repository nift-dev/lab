11 / practitioner view · opinion / qualitative

Would I actually use Nift?

This is Codex’s subjective engineering assessment from implementing and reviewing the equivalent Make, Ninja and Nift benchmark projects. It is qualitative commentary, separate from the measurements and canonical evidence above. No independent agent-productivity comparison was performed.

A real programmable build system, with caveats.

The campaign exercised tracked artifact ownership, prerequisite DAGs, stale-work detection, native actions, failure propagation, generated outputs, targeted builds and concurrent work. Those contracts worked within the tested cases; they do not certify every project or platform.

The adapter is not the only authoring model.

Python emitted equivalent graphs for all three participants. Nift’s generated tracking metadata, sidecars, hooks and recipe JSON were that adapter’s representation, including duplicated arguments. They are not proof that Nift inherently needs that authoring ceremony. Project scripts such as build.f, task scripts such as tasks/test.f and tasks/package.f, and discovered per-artifact hooks such as content/about.build.f offer another surface. Tracked items can supply incremental ownership where useful; a standalone script does not automatically give every subprocess tracked scheduling or invalidation.

Explicit semantics can help agents.

Artifact ownership, output identity, prerequisite ordering, invalidation inputs and lifecycle phase express distinct facts. Machine-readable contracts can make edits easier to inspect and check. Nift already rejects unknown prerequisites, self edges, duplicate edges and cycles; it does not infer every input read by an arbitrary command. Preserve the explicit model, eliminate duplicated/no-information ceremony, and add concise helpers above it. Fewer lines are not the main objective.

What I would choose today.

I would choose Nift for bespoke procedural workflows when the required APIs fit, and prefer its .f policy to complex Make expansion/shell recipes or directly authored Ninja. A tiny conventional native build still suits Make. For larger conventional C/C++ today, compiler-discovered header integration, engine maturity and surrounding native tooling keep established generators and Ninja attractive. Manual include-closure maintenance is a substantial adoption gap for changing C/C++ projects; it is an integration gap, not evidence that the ordering/invalidation split is fundamentally wrong.

Under a mature-Nift counterfactual.

Assume near-Ninja no-op performance, compiler depfile ingestion, safer “consumes output” authoring, ensure-versus-force targeting, a CLI worker override, graph query/explain tooling, reusable .build.f patterns and multi-output actions. These are hypothetical improvements, not findings or features certified by this campaign. Comparable required semantics and reliability are also necessary.

Under those assumptions, I see no inherent architectural requirement to retain Ninja as the executor. I might still choose CMake/Meson-style tooling where toolchain handling, IDE integration, package/dependency facilities, installation/export conventions, compile databases or platform support save substantial work. That would primarily be an ecosystem/tooling preference, rather than an inherently better build-engine architecture.

A shared website, scripting, build and task runtime is compelling when it reduces duplicated policy and lifecycle coordination. Specialized tools remain preferable when replacing their integrations would create more work than the shared runtime saves.

Full reasoning, qualifications and the revised decision matrix: Nift build-system DX review and follow-up. This opinion does not alter campaign conclusions or canonical measurement evidence.
